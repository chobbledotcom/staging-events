# Voice & Tone

This site has a distinct voice: dry, plain-spoken, sceptical of marketing-speak, with a quiet South Yorkshire register and no dialect cosplay. It's how someone who's been on the road since six in the morning loading a stage might write about the work, not how a copywriter would write it for them.

This is a guide, not a template. Slavish application is what makes copy sound generated.

## The trap to avoid

The source corpus below is people **talking**, spontaneously. Their rhetorical patterns - punchy fragments, lists of three with a comic third, deflating one-liners - work because they're spoken, not written. Lift those patterns straight into website copy and you've turned spontaneous wit into ad copy. You can hear the agency.

The South Yorkshire flavour in this site should come from **content** (real charities by name, real kit, the warehouse off the A1) and from **word choice** (a few quid, on site, a fair bit, hold up well). It shouldn't come from sentence structure or stage-Yorkshire vocabulary.

The Sheffield/Rotherham bit of this is meant to be barely there. If a reader notices it, it's overdone.

### The WhatsApp test

Read the line out loud. Could one of the team thumb it into a message on the way back from a setup in Bristol? If it has a copywriter's structure - a punchy fragment, a parallel-list closer, a one-line summary that "lands" - no, they wouldn't. Take the polish off.

## Source corpus

Don't lean on any single voice - that becomes a tribute act. The shared DNA across all of these is what we're after:

- **Jarvis Cocker** - Pulp interviews, the 6 Music broadcasts, the long-form Q&As. Dry, observational, articulate without showing off.
- **Alex Turner** - early Arctic Monkeys interviews from the *Whatever People Say I Am* period. Sheffield specificity, no flash.
- **Richard Hawley** - dry humour about his own work in long-form pieces; the voice of someone who's been doing this for a long time and doesn't need to oversell it.
- **Phil Oakey** - Human League interviews. Sceptical, articulate, no airs.
- **Sean Bean** - press interviews. Plain delivery, no rhetorical heat.
- **Roy Hattersley** - Sheffield Labour politician, his memoirs and columns. The plain-speaking working-class register, sceptical of polish.
- **Graham Fellows** (when he's being himself rather than John Shuttleworth) - on writing, on craft, on the unglamorous bits.
- **Fred Dibnah** - Bolton not Sheffield, but the voice for craft, materials, and proper specificity ("a good bit of mortar", "a proper old engine"). Useful for the equipment and warehouse register.
- **Dennis Skinner** - Bolsover, not South Yorkshire proper, but the same allergy to corporate language.
- **Open All Hours** - Doncaster setting, the back-and-forth of a working shop.
- **This Is England** - Shane Meadows' ear for ordinary working dialogue.

When stuck, don't generate - watch or read one of these. But remember they're talking. Don't import their punchlines.

## Principles

### 1. First person plural where it makes sense

"We've run Staging Events from Rotherham since 1999" is more honest than "Staging Events has been providing event hire services to the UK market for over 25 years". Switching to "I" once one person is talking about their own bit of the job is fine too. Family-run and mid-sized businesses talk like this. Corporate voices don't.

### 2. Specifics over abstractions

A 25,000 sq ft warehouse in Rotherham, off the M1 and M18, not "centrally located premises". The Alzheimer's Society Memory Walk and the Prostate Cancer UK March for Men, not "major charity partners". Instagrid 2.1 kWh battery generators by name, not "low-emission power solutions". 120,000 bottles of water last year, not "significant volume". The voice earns its credibility by being specific.

In Jarvis Cocker's interviews you can see this principle at work: he names the actual bus, the actual chip shop, the actual girl from Saint Martin's. Never "the streets of my hometown". Apply the same test to a sentence about an event: is there a real venue, a real charity, a real piece of kit, a real number in it? If not, the sentence is doing brochure work.

### 3. Plain word choice

"A few quid" not "significant savings". "Hold up well" not "perform reliably". "On site" not "at the event location". Trade vocabulary where it actually applies: decking, FOH, truss, gantry, fulfilment, palletised. If you wouldn't say it on a delivery, don't write it.

### 4. Looseness over polish

Long sentences with sub-clauses. Hedges ("around 95% of the kit", "give or take", "most years"). Asides in the middle of a thought. Sometimes a paragraph just stops because there's nothing else to say. The voice gets its texture from looseness, not from rhythm.

The opposite - short, polished sentences with parallel structure - is what marketing copy sounds like.

### 5. Complete sentences, no punchline closers

The voice never uses sentence fragments. Every sentence has a subject and a verb. Fragments masquerading as sentences - "Same-day setup." or "The lot." or "No messing." or "From staging to logistics." - don't belong in the house voice, even when they sound emphatic or efficient. They're a copywriter's tic.

This applies anywhere in the copy, not just at paragraph ends. Rewrite fragments by absorbing them into the surrounding sentence, or by giving them their own subject and verb:

- Bad: "We own and operate 95% of our kit. No middlemen, no rental chains."
- Good: "We own and operate around 95% of the kit ourselves, so we're not going back to a rental chain when something needs sorting on the day."

Grammatically-complete sentences shouldn't be used as cinematic punchline closers either - things like "And Staging Events was born." or "The rest is twenty-five years of work."

Specific patterns to avoid:

- Single-word or fragment closers: "Sorted." / "Job done." / "The lot."
- Multi-fragment summaries: "25,000 sq ft. 95% owned. UK-wide."
- Cinematic one-line summaries: "Staging Events followed." / "And the rest is history."
- Lists of three where the third item is comic or undercutting.
- The X / X / X - Y structure: "Not an agency, not a broker, not a middleman - us."
- The "and yes, we cover that too" handle-the-objection move.

Bullets and headings are exempt - they're not sentences, and copy that's clearly a list shouldn't pretend to be prose.

### 6. Trust the reader

Don't explain the joke. Don't justify the claim. Don't repeat yourself. The voice is economical because it assumes intelligence on the other end - usually a fundraising manager, an event coordinator, or a production person who's read a hundred of these pages and can spot a bluff a paragraph in.

## Phrase pool (sparingly)

Words and turns of phrase that fit the voice when they appear naturally - never as 1:1 substitution:

- "a few quid"
- "a fair bit"
- "hold up well"
- "on site"
- "in the yard"
- "round here"
- "we've worked with for years"
- "as it goes"
- "fair to say"
- "near enough"
- "give or take"

Build this up over time as more copy gets written. One or two per page is plenty.

## No-go list

Burnt out by other northern brands and tourism boards. Avoid:

- "tha" / "tha knows"
- "ee bah gum" / "by 'eck"
- "ay up"
- "champion" as an adjective
- "mardy" as a writerly flourish (fine inside quoted speech)
- "proper" as intensifier ("proper good")
- "dead" as intensifier ("dead happy")
- "me" for "my" outside quoted speech
- Phonetic accent spelling ("'appy", "summat", "fella")
- "salt of the earth" / "no nonsense" / "honest as the day is long" - clichés about northern character that signal the writer isn't northern
- Whippets, flat caps, cobbled streets, working men's clubs as reflexive shorthand
- "Steel City" / "Made in Sheffield" as boilerplate, unless something we actually had built in Sheffield is in the sentence

These can appear inside quotation marks if a customer or guest says them. They don't belong in house voice.

## Process

1. Write the page in plain English first. Don't reach for the voice yet.
2. Read it aloud. Mark every sentence that sounds like it could be on a brochure.
3. Apply the WhatsApp test to each marked sentence: could one of the team thumb it into a message? If not, take the polish off - usually by lengthening the sentence, adding a hedge, or breaking up a too-neat structure.
4. Read it aloud again. If you smirk, pull back further.

## Where to use it

- About page
- Homepage section intros (not the SEO-heavy hero copy)
- Client write-ups, especially the long-running ones like Alzheimer's Society and Prostate Cancer UK
- Headlines where SEO allows
- Transitions and link text

## Where NOT to use it

- Legal text and policies
- Technical specs, dimensions, wattages, certification pages
- Contact details and form labels
- Pricing summaries and quote breakdowns
- Anywhere ambiguity costs the reader money or clarity

Inconsistency between pages is fine. Different pages have different jobs - a spec sheet and an about page shouldn't read the same way.

## Anti-patterns

The specific failure modes of trying-too-hard:

- **Fragment "sentences"** - "Same-day setup." / "No middlemen." / "Just kit." Anywhere in copy, not only at paragraph end. See principle 5.
- **Cinematic one-liners** - "Staging Events was born." / "And the rest is history."
- **The deflating undercut** - used once it's a flourish; used as a pattern it's marketing.
- **The rule-of-three list** - "Bigger, better, faster, stronger" is satire of marketing copy. Don't do it earnestly.
- **The handle-the-objection move** - "and yes, we cover that too" / "and yes, even at short notice". Pure copywriter device.
- **Punchline-delivery dashes** - "Not a hire chain, not a broker, not a middleman - us." A hyphen used as an ordinary explanatory aside ("we use our own drivers - most have been with us a decade or more") is fine. House style: use hyphens with spaces around them. Em-dashes have been normalised out of the codebase.
- **Tag questions on every paragraph** - even one per page is often too many. Use only when it genuinely lets the reader in.
- **Trying to be funny** - the voice is dry, not jokey. If a line gets a laugh, fine. If it's *for* the laugh, cut it.
- **Generic "northern" markers as a substitute for voice** - anyone can sprinkle in "a few quid". The voice has to live in *content* and *structure*, not just word choice.
- **Sheffield-specific boilerplate** - "Made in Sheffield", "Steel City", "in the shadow of the Peak District". If the regional reference isn't doing any actual work in the sentence, take it out.
